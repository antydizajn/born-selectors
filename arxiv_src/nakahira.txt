                                        Appendix A: Operational-probabilistic theories (OPTs)

    A theory satisfying all assumptions stated in this section will be called an operational-probabilistic theory (OPT). This no-
tion is essentially the same as the causal operational-probabilistic framework described in Chaps. II–VI of Ref. [9], with the
assumption of local discriminability omitted; the presentation below is slightly different. An OPT generalizes the operational
and probabilistic features of quantum theory and is closely related to what are often called generalized probabilistic theories.
    Throughout, natural numbers are positive integers. Let R and C denote the sets of real and complex numbers, respectively.
Let R≥0 denote the set of nonnegative real numbers, and let [0, 1] denote the set of real numbers between 0 and 1, inclusive. Let
Cn denote the set of n-dimensional complex column vectors, and let Cn×n denote the set of complex square matrices of order n.
For each natural number n, let Hern and Her+n denote the sets of complex Hermitian matrices of order n and positive semidefinite
matrices of order n, respectively. Let Denn denote the set of density matrices of order n, namely Denn B {ρ ∈ Her+n | tr ρ = 1}.
The real vector space spanned by Denn is Hern . The identity matrix of order n is denoted by In . All real vector spaces appearing
in this paper are assumed to be finite dimensional. For a real vector space V and a convex cone C ⊆ V, the dual cone of C,
denoted by C∗ , is the set { f ∈ V∗ | f (x) ≥ 0 (∀x ∈ C)}, where V∗ is the dual vector space of V; this is again a convex cone. For
convex sets X and Y and the real vector spaces V and W that they span, a linear isomorphism satisfying f (X) = Y, written as
 f : V → W, will be called, by abuse of terminology, a linear isomorphism from X onto Y. If such a linear isomorphism exists,
X and Y are called linearly isomorphic, or simply isomorphic, and we write X  Y. In particular, a linear isomorphism from X
to itself is called a linear automorphism of X.


                                                        1.       Framework of OPTs

   We assume that there are objects called systems, denoted by symbols such as A, B, C, . . . . Let Sys be the set of all systems.
For every pair of systems A and B, there are objects called tests from A to B. A test F from A to B is a finite collection { fi }ki=1 ,
where k may depend on the test, and each element fi is called a transformation. Intuitively, performing the test F produces a
classical outcome i ∈ {1, . . . , k} and applies the corresponding transformation fi . Although Ref. [9] allows more general outcome
sets, we restrict attention to finite-outcome tests only to simplify notation; all constructions and proofs below use only this finite-
outcome fragment, so this convention entails no essential change in the present arguments. The set of all transformations from
A to B is denoted by Trans(A, B). A transformation f is called deterministic, or a channel, if the singleton collection { f } is a test.
A test from A to A is often called a test on A, and the same convention is used for transformations and channels.
   There is a special system I, called the trivial system, such that every probability distribution (i.e., every collection {pi ∈ R≥0 }ki=1
satisfying ki=1 pi = 1) is a test from I to I, and there are no other tests from I to I. Hence Trans(I, I) = [0, 1]. Elements of

further operational postulates to local discriminability and purification in a causal OPT [12]. Although the present definition of
purification differs slightly as explained above, the present work addresses this motivation more directly by deriving standard
finite-dimensional quantum theory from only local equivalence, equivalently local discriminability, and ES purification.


                                            Appendix C: Derivation of symmetric cones

  In what follows, we derive quantum theory from local equivalence and ES purification. The argument has four main steps.

   (1) The state cone St+ (A) is shown to be symmetric for every A (Theorem 28).
   (2) The no-restriction hypothesis is established (Theorem 35); the hypothesis is explained at the beginning of Sec. C 4.
   (3) The normalized state space is identified with DennA for every A, namely StN (A)  DennA (Theorem 45).
   (4) These results are combined to conclude that the OPT is quantum theory (Theorem 51).

Throughout the rest of this supplement, we work in a fixed OPT satisfying local equivalence and ES purification.
   In a real vector space V, a convex cone C is homogeneous if it has interior points and, for any two interior points x and y of
C, there exists a linear automorphism Γ x,y of C such that Γ x,y (x) = y. In a real inner-product space V, a convex cone C ⊆ V is
self-dual if {y ∈ V | ⟨y, x⟩ ≥ 0 (∀x ∈ C)} is equal to C. A cone in a real inner-product space is called a symmetric cone if it is
homogeneous and self-dual. This section proves that every state cone St+ (A) is symmetric.

                                                  3.    Symmetric cones and Euclidean Jordan algebras

   By Theorem 28, St+ (A) is a symmetric cone. It is well known that the real vector space spanned by a symmetric cone can
be regarded as a Euclidean Jordan algebra (EJA). This subsection recalls how the vector space spanned by St+ (A) is naturally
associated with an EJA, and records basic facts about symmetric cones and EJAs. For proofs, see for example Ref. [46].
   An EJA is a finite-dimensional real inner-product space E equipped with a bilinear product • and a unit element u such that,
for all x and y in E, x • y = y • x, x • (x2 • y) = x2 • (x • y) where x2 B x • x, u • x = x, and

                                                            ⟨x • y, z⟩ = ⟨y, x • z⟩ ,      ∀x, y, z ∈ E.                                                     (C8)

The positive cone of E is E+ B {x2 | x ∈ E}. This cone is symmetric. Conversely, as recalled below, for every symmetric cone
C, there exists an EJA E with C = E+ . If x and y are in E and satisfy y − x ∈ E+ , we write x ≤ y or y ≥ x.
   An element p ∈ E with p • p = p is called an idempotent. A nonzero idempotent p is primitive if there do not exist nonzero
idempotents p1 and p2 with p1 + p2 = p and p1 • p2 = 0. For a convex cone C, a half-line R≥0 x B {cx | c ∈ R≥0 } generated
by nonzero x ∈ C is an extreme ray of C if x = y + z with y and z in C implies that y and z are in R≥0 x. We also say that such
x lies on an extreme ray of C. For every primitive idempotent p, R≥0 p is an extreme ray of E+ , and every extreme ray of E+ is
of this form. Elements x and y in E are called orthogonal, written x ⊥ y, if x • y = 0. A family of primitive idempotents {pi }ri=1
satisfying ri=1 pi = u is called a Jordan frame. The natural number r is uniquely determined and called the rank of E. Elements
           P
of a Jordan frame are mutually orthogonal. Any family of mutually orthogonal primitive idempotents has size at most r and can
be extended to a Jordan frame. In particular, a mutually orthogonal family of r primitive idempotents is already a Jordan frame.

elements.

                                                  2.   Derivation of quantum theory

   We have now proved the no-restriction hypothesis by Theorem 35 and StN (A)  DennA by Theorem 45. It remains to derive
quantum theory from these facts, which is not difficult. There are several possible routes for this derivation, for example those
in Refs. [9, 10, 12, 26]. Here we describe the route that applies Theorem 19, “states specify the theory”, of Ref. [9].
   The trace tr on Hern is the usual matrix trace. For every system A, a linear isomorphism S A : StR (A) → HernA satisfying
S A (StN (A)) = DennA will be called a representation of A. Since S A preserves the trace, tr ◦S A = trA . A linear isomorphism
preserving the inner product on a complex Hilbert space will be called unitary. For a unitary matrix V of order n, write AdV for
the map Hern ∋ X 7→ V XV † ∈ Hern . The map AdV is a linear automorphism of Denn , and therefore preserves the trace, namely
tr ◦ AdV = tr. Fix an orthonormal basis for each Hern , and let T : Hern ∋ X 7→ X T ∈ Hern denote the corresponding transposition.
Since this transposition is a linear automorphism of Denn , if S A is a representation of A, then so is T ◦ S A . Put Hn B Cn , and
denote by Pn the set of all trace-one rank-one projections in Hern .
   We define uniqueness of purification as follows.

Definition 46 (Uniqueness of purification) The theory is said to satisfy uniqueness of purification if, for every pair of systems
A and B, whenever normalized pure states Ψ and Ψ′ of AB are both purifications of ρ ∈ StN (A), there exists a reversible channel
U on B such that Ψ′ = (idA ⊗ U)(Ψ).

  Then the following proposition holds.
                                                                                                                                                  26

Proposition 47 If an OPT satisfying local equivalence and ES purification also satisfies uniqueness of purification, then it is
quantum theory.

Proof Let Θ be an OPT satisfying local equivalence and ES purification. By Theorem 45, for every system A, there exists a
trace-preserving linear isomorphism S A : StR (A) → HernA such that S A (StN (A)) = DennA . We use these isomorphisms to identify
each StN (A) with DennA . Under this identification, a transformation f ∈ Trans(A, B) is represented by S B ◦ f ◦ S −1A , and tests
are represented by applying this replacement to each outcome. Thus the resulting isomorphic copy of Θ and the comparison
quantum theory have the same set of normalized states for every system A, in the sense required by Theorem 19 of Ref. [9].
The comparison quantum theory is taken on the same system labels, with level nA assigned to system A; this is compatible
with composition because Lemma 42 gives nAB = nA nB . Since Θ satisfies ES purification and uniqueness of purification, this
isomorphic copy satisfies the purification principle of Ref. [9]: existence follows from ES purification, and uniqueness for
arbitrary common purifying systems follows from the uniqueness-of-purification assumption. Quantum theory also satisfies
the purification principle. Theorem 19 of Ref. [9] is stated in the framework of that paper. In the present terminology, this
is the framework of OPTs satisfying local discriminability. The theorem says that, if two OPTs satisfying purification and
local discriminability have the same normalized state space for every system, then the two theories coincide, including their
transformations and tests. As noted above, local discriminability is equivalent to local equivalence, and the comparison quantum
theory satisfies local discriminability. Therefore the theorem applies to the relabelled theory and the comparison quantum theory.
